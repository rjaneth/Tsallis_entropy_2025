\begin{reviewbox}{Comment 1 }
The core of the proposed method relies on Tsallis entropy, which introduces an additional entropic index $q$. However, the manuscript fails to provide a clear physical justification for the choice of $q$ in the context of SAR backscattering. The authors should explain how specific values of $q$ relate to different degrees of non-additivity in radar returns, or how they correspond to specific physical clutter models (e.g., $K$-distribution or $\mathcal{G}^{0}$ distribution).
\end{reviewbox}

\begin{reviewbox}{Comment 2}
While the bootstrap correction is intended to improve accuracy for small sample sizes, the paper does not discuss the computational cost associated with performing bootstrap resampling for every pixel or local window. Furthermore, in highly heterogeneous regions, the bootstrap might inadvertently sample across structural boundaries, leading to biased estimates. A more thorough analysis of the bootstrap’s variance reduction versus its potential bias in edge regions is needed.
\end{reviewbox}

\begin{reviewbox}{Comment 3}
The manuscript claims that the proposed tool does not require ``scene-specific calibration.'' However, nonparametric entropy estimators are known to be sensitive to the quantization of the input data (bit depth) and to any prior despeckling filters applied. The authors should include a sensitivity analysis showing how the $p$-value maps change when the input SAR data are scaled or filtered differently.
\end{reviewbox}

\begin{reviewbox}{Comment 4}
In the Related Work section, the authors focus heavily on classical information theory and basic statistical SAR modeling. While these are foundational, the discussion lacks a connection to contemporary SAR target detection and anomaly detection methodologies (2024--2025), such as ORPDet, PDE-guided approaches, BurgsVO, and S4Det.
\end{reviewbox}

\begin{responsebox}{Response}

In recent years, deep learning-based methods have demonstrated superior performance in automatic feature extraction for SAR imagery, particularly in ship detection. For instance, \cite{ORPSDOuterRectangularProjectionBasedRepresentationforOrientedShipDetectioninSARImages} proposes a methodology that incorporates an outer rectangular projection and a convex quadrilateral rectification scheme to feed a lightweight fully convolutional network. Additionally, the integration of partial differential equations into neural network architectures has yielded promising improvements in vessel identification \cite{PDEGuidedDiverseFeatureLearningforSARRotatedShipDetection, BurgsVOBurgsAssociatedVertexOffsetEncodingSchemeforDetectingRotatedShipsinSARImages, S4DetBreadthandAccurateSineSingleStageShipDetectionforRemoteSenseSARImagery}.

Techniques such as convolutional neural networks, U-Net architectures, and transformer-based models leverage hierarchical feature representations learned from large annotated datasets, enabling robust detection even under complex clutter conditions.
Nevertheless, deep learning algorithms present three primary limitations: \emph{(i)} their applicability is often restricted, as they require large volumes of labeled SAR data, rendering them costly and time-consuming; \emph{(ii)} they lack physical interpretability, complicating the validation of decision processes against known statistical properties of SAR imagery; and \emph{(iii)} they are highly sensitive to domain shift, as networks trained on a specific configuration frequently generalize poorly to images acquired under disparate conditions.

In contrast, the proposed method directly exploits the statistical properties of SAR images through the use of Tsallis entropy as a segmentation criterion. 
This choice is physically motivated by the fact that SAR images are well characterized by heavy-tailed distributions (such as the $\mathcal{G}^0$ family), while the non-extensive parameter $\lambda$ of the Tsallis entropy provides a flexible mechanism to adapt the measure to different levels of scene heterogeneity. 
Consequently, the approach is inherently generalizable across sensors, resolutions, and target types, requiring no retraining or hyperparameter tuning beyond the selection of $\lambda$.
Furthermore, methods based on entropic thresholding and region analysis are fully transparent and interpretable: the segmentation criterion has a closed-form expression and a clear statistical meaning, allowing practitioners to audit and validate results.
Rather than competing with deep learning approaches, the proposed method offers a suitable alternative for scenarios where labeled data are scarce or unavailable, where physical interpretability is required, or where computational resources are constrained.


\end{responsebox}
